## Overview

This lesson turns the interview transcript into a practical framework for evaluating a supply shock thesis. The central claim in the source is not that the US is literally running out of gas underground, but that a modeled mismatch could emerge between 2028 and 2030 among natural gas production growth, LNG export commitments, AI-related power demand, and the infrastructure needed to process, move, and store gas. Treat this as a scenario analysis built from the speaker’s modeling assumptions, not as a settled forecast. The useful takeaway is how to reason from resource base, to flow capacity, to infrastructure bottlenecks, to end-user price exposure.

## Key Concepts

- **Resource vs. deliverability**: The source distinguishes between gas existing underground and gas that can actually reach customers on time. The speaker argues the risk is mainly about flow capacity and timing, not immediate geological exhaustion.
- **Demand layering**: The thesis depends on stacking multiple demand sources: existing domestic use, rising LNG exports, and incremental gas demand tied to AI/data centers and power generation. The warning is that each new project may look manageable alone while the combined system becomes tight.
- **Midstream constraints**: Processing plants, gathering systems, and interstate pipelines are presented as critical bottlenecks. Even if wells can be drilled, gas still must be processed to pipeline specifications and physically transported to where it is needed.
- **Storage as the balancing mechanism**: Working gas storage is described as the place where supply-demand imbalance shows up first. In the speaker’s model, historically low storage levels in 2028-2030 are the main signal that the system is moving from tightness toward crisis.
- **Forward curves can hide structural risk**: The transcript argues that current market prices may remain complacent because near-term supply looks fine and later-dated markets are illiquid. A flat forward curve does not prove long-term abundance if structural demand arrives before supply and infrastructure adjust.
- **Convex price outcomes**: When a system has little slack, small additional shortages can create very large price moves. The speaker repeatedly frames late-decade gas and electricity prices as potentially convex rather than linear.
- **Scenario-dependent winners and losers**: Within the interview’s thesis, upstream gas producers, some solar assets, and large-scale nuclear supply chains could benefit, while consumers, gas-dependent power buyers without secured fuel, and some distributed generation manufacturers could be exposed.
- **Mitigation by time horizon**: Shorter-term mitigations in the source include building processing and pipelines, securing physical gas, and expanding solar deployment. Longer-term mitigation centers on building large nuclear plants, which the speaker treats as necessary because they take many years to deliver.

## How It Works

Use the transcript’s logic as a reusable analysis method. First, separate stock questions from flow questions: ask not only how much resource exists, but how much can be produced, processed, transported, and stored by a given date. Second, map demand by category rather than treating it as one number; include legacy demand, export obligations, and new loads such as AI power. Third, identify the actual bottlenecks in sequence: wells, processing, gathering, pipelines, storage, and generation. Fourth, check whether market prices reflect those bottlenecks or merely current comfort. Fifth, stress-test outcomes under constrained supply, including price spikes, contract stress, and political tradeoffs between exports, industrial growth, and household bills. In the source, this process leads to a late-2020s tightness thesis, but the broader lesson is methodological: infrastructure systems fail at their narrowest constraint, not at their largest headline resource number.

## Training Exercise

Build a one-page stress test for any energy-intensive project. Define the project’s fuel or power need, then list five dependency layers: source production, processing, transportation, storage, and end-use delivery. For each layer, write one sentence on current capacity, one sentence on what must expand, and one sentence on what happens if expansion arrives two years late. Finish by answering three questions: What assumption is doing the most work in the forecast? What indicator would tell you the thesis is starting to play out? What backup plan reduces exposure if the core input price triples?
